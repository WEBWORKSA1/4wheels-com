How auto loans work

Learn how auto loans work: principal, APR vs interest rate, how loan term changes total cost, credit scores, lender types, pre-approval and gap insurance.

By 4Wheels Editorial TeamUpdated October 28, 20267 min readFinance

An auto loan is a fixed amount of money you borrow to buy a car and repay in equal monthly payments over a set term, usually three to seven years. Each payment covers that month's interest plus a slice of the principal, and the car serves as collateral until the loan is paid off.

Three numbers decide what a loan really costs: the amount you borrow, the APR and the term. Understand how they interact and you can compare offers in minutes and spot a bad deal before you sign.

Principal, interest and how payments work

The principal is the amount you borrow: the car's price plus taxes, fees and any add-ons you finance, minus your down payment and trade-in. The lender charges interest on whatever balance is still outstanding.

Most US auto loans are simple-interest loans with a fixed payment calculated by amortization. Early in the loan, more of each payment goes to interest because the balance is at its highest. On a $30,000 loan at 7% APR for 60 months, the payment is about $594.04. In the first month, $175 goes to interest and about $419 to principal. By the final year, almost the entire payment reduces the balance.

Lenders also care about the loan-to-value ratio, which compares the loan amount with the car's value. A bigger down payment lowers it, which can mean a better rate and less risk of owing more than the car is worth. APR vs interest rate

The interest rate is the annual cost of borrowing the principal. The annual percentage rate (APR) rolls in certain finance charges, such as some lender fees, so it is the better number for comparing offers. If two loans have the same interest rate but one carries a loan fee, that one will show a higher APR.

In the US, lenders must disclose the APR, the finance charge, the amount financed and the total of payments before you sign. Compare offers on APR and total of payments, never on monthly payment alone. A low payment can hide a long term, a high rate or extra products folded into the loan.

Before you sign any loan, confirm:

  • The APR and term match the offer you accepted.
  • The amount financed matches the agreed price minus your down payment and trade-in.
  • No add-ons you declined have been folded into the loan.
  • You know whether there is a prepayment penalty.
  • The total of payments matches your own calculation.

How loan term changes what you pay

Stretching the term lowers your monthly payment but raises the total interest, because you carry the balance longer. Here is the same $30,000 loan at 7% APR over five common terms:

TermMonthly paymentTotal interestTotal paid
36 months$926.31$3,347$33,347
48 months$718.39$4,483$34,483
60 months$594.04$5,642$35,642
72 months$511.47$6,826$36,826
84 months$452.78$8,034$38,034

Going from 60 to 84 months saves about $141 a month but costs about $2,391 more in interest. In real life, the gap is often wider, because lenders usually charge a higher APR on longer terms. Long loans also keep you "underwater" for longer, since cars typically lose value fastest in their first few years.

Key takeaway: Choose the shortest term whose payment fits comfortably in your budget. If a car only works at 72 or 84 months, consider a less expensive car or a larger down payment.

How your credit score affects your rate

Lenders price loans by risk, and your credit score is their main signal. Borrowers with strong scores get the lowest APRs. As scores drop into fair and poor ranges, rates climb, and borrowers in the lowest tiers can pay several times what top-tier borrowers do. Lenders also weigh your income, existing debts, the loan term and the age of the car. Used-car loans usually carry higher rates than new-car loans.

To see how much the rate matters, take a $30,000 loan for 60 months. At 5% APR, the payment is about $566 and total interest is about $3,968. At 9%, it is about $623 and $7,365. At 14%, it is about $698 and $11,883. Those rates are illustrations, not quotes. Your actual rate depends on your profile and the market.

Before you shop, check your credit reports for errors and avoid opening new credit accounts. Even a modest score improvement can move you into a cheaper pricing tier.

Where to get an auto loan

Lender typeWhat to know
Dealer-arranged financingConvenient, and sometimes the only way to get manufacturer promotional rates on new cars. The dealer may add a markup to the rate the lender approved, so compare it with outside offers.
BanksOften competitive for borrowers with good credit, and existing customers may get a discount.
Credit unionsMember-owned and often among the lowest rates. You need to qualify for membership, which is often easy.
Online lendersQuick applications and fast decisions, and many let you check rates with a soft credit pull first.

Outside the US, the options differ. Many UK buyers use hire purchase or personal contract purchase (PCP), and in Canada and India, dealer and bank financing dominate. Disclosure rules and rate caps vary by country, state and province, so check what applies where you live.

Pre-approval and the rate-shopping window

A pre-approval is a lender's conditional offer of a specific amount and APR. Getting one before you shop sets a firm budget, separates the financing from the price negotiation, and gives the dealer a rate to beat. Use the affordability calculator to decide how much to request.

  1. Check your credit reports and scores.
  2. Apply with two or three lenders, such as a credit union, a bank and an online lender, for the same amount and term.
  3. Keep every application within about two weeks.
  4. Compare APR, term, fees and total of payments, then bring your best offer to the dealer.

Credit scoring models generally count multiple auto loan inquiries within a short window, typically 14 to 45 days depending on the model, as a single inquiry. The CFPB explains this in its answer on how shopping for an auto loan affects your credit.

Negative equity, gap insurance and paying early

Negative equity

You have negative equity when you owe more than the car is worth. It's common with small down payments and long terms. If you trade in an underwater car, the dealer may roll the shortfall into your new loan. Adding $3,000 of negative equity to a $30,000 loan at 7% for 60 months raises the payment from about $594 to $653 and the interest from about $5,642 to $6,206, and you start the new loan even deeper underwater.

Gap insurance

If your car is totaled or stolen, your insurer pays its actual cash value, which can be less than your loan balance. Gap coverage pays the difference. It's worth considering if you put down less than about 20% or take a long term. Compare the price from your auto insurer or lender with the dealer's price before you buy it.

Paying early

On a simple-interest loan, paying extra toward principal cuts your interest. Adding $100 a month to the $30,000, 7%, 60-month loan pays it off in 50 months and saves about $972 in interest.

Watch out: Check your contract for prepayment penalties and for precomputed interest, which some subprime loans use and which reduces what you save by paying early. Ask the lender to apply extra payments to principal, not to future payments.

If rates fall or your credit improves after you buy, see our guide to refinancing a car loan and run the numbers in the refinance calculator.

The bottom line

An auto loan's true cost comes down to the amount borrowed, the APR and the term. Borrow as little as you reasonably can, choose the shortest term you can afford, get pre-approved by a few lenders in a short window, and compare every offer on APR and total of payments. The CFPB's auto loan resources have worksheets to help. This guide is general information, not financial or legal advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good APR for a car loan?

It depends on your credit, the loan term, whether the car is new or used, and current market rates. The best way to know what is good for you is to get pre-approved by two or three lenders and compare their APRs for the same amount and term.

Is it better to take a longer loan for a lower payment?

A longer term lowers the monthly payment but raises the total interest you pay and keeps you owing more than the car is worth for longer. A $30,000 loan at 7% APR costs about $5,642 in interest over 60 months and about $8,034 over 84 months.

Does applying with several lenders hurt my credit?

Credit scoring models generally count multiple auto loan inquiries made within a short window, typically 14 to 45 days depending on the model, as a single inquiry. Do your rate shopping within a couple of weeks to stay safe.

Can I pay off my car loan early?

Most auto loans allow it, and paying early saves interest on a simple-interest loan. Check your contract for any prepayment penalty, and make sure extra payments are applied to principal.
