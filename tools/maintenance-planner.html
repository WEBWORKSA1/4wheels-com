    How to use this maintenance planner

    1. Enter your car's current odometer reading.
    2. Enter roughly how many miles you drive a year.
    3. Select the powertrain: gas, hybrid, diesel or electric.
    4. Choose normal or severe driving conditions.
    5. Review the services due in the next 12 months and compare them with your owner's manual.

    How it's calculated

    The planner looks ahead by one year of driving, from your current mileage to current mileage plus your yearly miles, and lists each service whose interval falls within that range. It uses these typical intervals:

    • Oil and filter: 5,000 to 10,000 miles, depending on oil type.
    • Tire rotation: every 6,000 miles.
    • Engine air filter: 20,000 miles. Cabin air filter: 15,000 miles.
    • Brake inspection: 12,000 miles. Brake fluid: every 3 years, or about 36,000 miles.
    • Coolant: 60,000 to 100,000 miles. Transmission fluid: 60,000 miles.
    • Spark plugs: 100,000 miles. Battery test: yearly once the car is past its third year.

    Severe conditions shorten these intervals by about a third, so a 6,000-mile rotation becomes roughly every 4,000 miles. Electric vehicles skip oil changes, spark plugs and the engine air filter.

    Example: your gas car shows 41,000 miles and you drive 12,000 miles a year, so the planner checks up to 53,000 miles. On full synthetic oil with a 10,000-mile interval and normal driving, it lists tire rotations at 42,000 and 48,000, a cabin filter at 45,000, a brake inspection at 48,000 and an oil change at 50,000. At 12,000 miles a year, the car is roughly three and a half years old, so a battery test is suggested too. The engine air filter and transmission fluid, both due at 60,000, fall beyond this window.

    Getting more from your maintenance budget

    • Check your owner's manual for both a mileage and a time limit. Fluids and rubber parts age even when you rarely drive.
    • Combine services when they come due close together to save repeat labor and trips.
    • Keep receipts and dates. A complete record supports warranty claims and helps resale value.
    • Replace engine and cabin air filters yourself if you're comfortable doing so. On many cars, it takes just a few minutes with basic tools.
    • Have a mechanic look at warning lights, new noises or changes in braking promptly, rather than waiting for the next scheduled visit.

    Good to know: Hybrids often go longer between brake jobs because regenerative braking does much of the stopping, but their brake fluid still needs changing on schedule. Always follow the intervals in your owner's manual for your specific vehicle.

    Frequently asked questions

    What counts as severe driving conditions?

    Most owner's manuals list frequent short trips, stop-and-go traffic, extreme heat or cold, dusty or unpaved roads, towing or carrying heavy loads, and long periods of idling. Many everyday drivers fall into this category without realizing it.

    Do electric cars need maintenance?

    Yes, but less of it. EVs skip oil changes, spark plugs and engine air filters. They still need tire rotations, cabin air filters, brake inspections, brake fluid and checks of the battery cooling system.

    Should I follow this planner or my owner's manual?

    Your owner's manual. This planner uses typical intervals to help you budget and plan, but manufacturers set schedules for each engine and transmission, and some cars have a maintenance monitor that adjusts reminders based on how you drive.
