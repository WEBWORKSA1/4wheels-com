The smartest way to buy a new car is to break the deal into separate pieces and settle them one at a time: your budget, the car, the financing, the price, your trade-in and the add-ons. Dealers make the most money when all of those pieces blur into a single monthly payment. Keep them apart and you will usually pay less.

This guide walks through each step in order, from setting a number before you shop to inspecting the car before you drive it home. Most of it applies anywhere, but taxes, fees and consumer protections vary by state, province and country, so check your local rules where noted.

Set your budget before you shop

Decide what you can afford before a salesperson asks what monthly payment you have in mind. Start with the total price you are comfortable financing, not the payment. Our affordability calculator works backward from your income, down payment and preferred loan term to a realistic price range.

One popular rule of thumb is 20/4/10: put at least 20% down, finance for no more than four years, and keep total car costs, including the payment, insurance and fuel, under about 10% of your gross income. You don't have to hit it exactly, but if a car only fits your budget at 72 or 84 months, it is probably more car than your budget supports.

Look past the loan, too. Insurance on a sporty or luxury model can cost far more than on a family sedan, and fuel, maintenance and depreciation add up quickly. The total cost of ownership calculator lets you compare models on five-year cost rather than sticker price.

Research the car, then the price

Narrow your list to two or three models and a specific trim. Check crash-test results from the NHTSA 5-Star Safety Ratings and the IIHS vehicle ratings, read owner reviews for reliability, and get an insurance quote on each finalist. Test-drive them back to back, ideally on roads you use every day.

Then learn the three prices that matter:

PriceWhat it meansHow to use it
MSRP (sticker price)The manufacturer's suggested retail price, shown on the window sticker along with a destination charge.A ceiling on most models, not a target. Scarce or very popular vehicles may sell at or above it.
Invoice priceWhat the manufacturer bills the dealer. The dealer's true cost is often lower once holdback and dealer incentives are counted.A rough floor. A price near or below invoice is usually a strong deal.

Also check the manufacturer's website for rebates, loyalty offers and promotional financing. These change monthly and sometimes force a choice between a cash rebate and a low APR, so run the numbers both ways.

Get pre-approved before the dealer

Apply for an auto loan with a bank, credit union or online lender before you visit a showroom. A pre-approval tells you your rate and maximum loan amount, and it gives the dealer a number to beat. If the finance office offers a lower APR with no strings attached, take it. If not, you already have your financing.

Rate shopping won't wreck your credit if you keep it tight, because scoring models generally treat several auto loan inquiries made within a short window as one. Negotiate the out-the-door price by email

The out-the-door price is the one number that matters: the vehicle price plus destination, dealer fees, taxes, title and registration. Negotiating it in writing with several dealers at once takes the pressure out of the process and makes offers easy to compare.

  1. Pick the exact model, trim, color and options you want, plus any acceptable alternatives.
  2. Contact the internet sales department at three to five dealers through their websites. Ask for an itemized out-the-door quote on a specific in-stock vehicle, including its VIN.
  3. Say plainly that you are comparing written quotes, that your financing is arranged, and that you will discuss any trade-in separately.
  4. Share the best quote with the other dealers and ask whether they can beat it. Stay polite and specific.
  5. When you accept an offer, ask for the buyer's order in writing before you go in, and bring a copy with you.

Read every quote line by line. Destination charges are rarely negotiable, but dealer-installed accessories, market adjustments and protection packages often are. Documentation fees vary widely; some states cap them and others don't, so compare them across dealers instead of assuming they are fixed.

Keep your trade-in separate

Bundling your trade-in with the purchase lets a dealer show you a generous trade value while quietly giving less off the new car, or the reverse. Settle the out-the-door price first, then bring up the trade-in as its own transaction.

Before that conversation, get written offers from at least one or two instant-offer buyers or other dealers so you know what your car is worth. If the dealer's number is lower, sell elsewhere. In many states, trading in reduces sales tax on the new car because tax is charged only on the difference, so a slightly lower dealer offer can still come out ahead. Check your state or provincial rules. Our guide to selling your car for the most money covers the options.

Get through the F&I office

After you agree on a price, you'll meet the finance and insurance (F&I) manager, who prepares the contract and sells optional products. These add-ons are a major profit center for dealers, and their prices are usually negotiable. You can decline any of them, and many can be bought later or elsewhere.

Add-onWhat to do
Extended warranty or service contractOptional. You can usually buy one later, before the factory warranty ends. Price-check it and negotiate if you want it.
Gap insuranceWorth considering with a small down payment or a long loan, but compare the price with your auto insurer or lender first.
Paint, fabric or undercoating protectionUsually decline. Much of it is a sealant you could apply yourself for far less.
VIN etching, nitrogen-filled tiresUsually decline, and question it if it appears pre-installed and labeled as mandatory.
Tire-and-wheel or key replacement plansOptional. Check what your insurance or roadside plan already covers.
Credit life or disability insuranceOptional. Federal law does not require it to get a car loan.

Watch out: If you are told an add-on is required to get the loan or the advertised rate, ask to see that requirement in writing from the lender. Also watch for products added to the contract that you never agreed to, and for a monthly payment higher than your own math predicts.

The FTC's consumer guide to buying a new car has more on add-ons and dealer financing.

Check the paperwork and inspect the car

Don't sign until every number matches what you agreed to. Take as long as you need.

  • The sale price, fees, taxes and out-the-door total match your written quote.
  • The APR, term, amount financed and monthly payment match your approval or the offer you accepted.
  • No add-ons appear that you declined.
  • The trade-in value and any payoff on your old loan are correct.
  • The VIN on the contract matches the car you are taking home.
  • Any promises, such as a second key or a scratch repair, are written on a "we owe" form.
  • The financing is final, not conditional on later approval, before you drive away.

Delivery inspection

Inspect the car in daylight before you leave, not after. Walk around it looking for scratches, dents and uneven panel gaps. Check that the odometer reading is low and ask about anything beyond normal test-drive and delivery miles. Confirm you have both keys, the owner's manual, floor mats and any accessories you paid for. Test the lights, screen, phone pairing, climate control, windows and driver-assistance features. If anything is wrong, have it noted in writing before you sign the delivery receipt.

Key takeaway: Settle one thing at a time, in this order: financing, the out-the-door price, your trade-in, then add-ons. Get every agreed number in writing before you sign.

The bottom line

Getting a good deal on a new car is mostly preparation. Know your budget, arrive with a pre-approval, compare written out-the-door quotes from several dealers, and treat your trade-in and every add-on as separate decisions. A calm, written, one-step-at-a-time process beats hours of showroom haggling, and it leaves far less room for surprises in the finance office.
