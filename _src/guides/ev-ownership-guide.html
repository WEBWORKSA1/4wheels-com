Owning an electric vehicle comes down to three habits: charging at home overnight, planning around real-world range rather than the sticker number, and knowing what your battery warranty covers. Get those right and an EV is usually cheaper to fuel and simpler to maintain than a gas car.

Here is how charging, plug standards, range, running costs and battery health work, plus what to check on a used EV.

Charging levels and how fast they work

Charging power is measured in kilowatts (kW). Miles added depend on your car's efficiency, so treat these as typical ranges.

LevelPower sourceTypical powerRange added
Level 1Standard 120 V outletAbout 1.2 to 1.9 kWRoughly 3 to 5 miles per hour
Level 2240 V at home, often 208 V in publicCommonly 6 to 11.5 kW at homeRoughly 15 to 40 miles per hour
DC fastCommercial stationsAbout 50 to 350 kWOften 100 to 200+ miles in 30 minutes

Your car's onboard charger caps Level 2 speed: a car limited to 7.2 kW won't charge faster on an 11 kW unit. DC fast charging is quickest when the battery is low and slows sharply above about 80%, so on road trips two short stops from 10% to 70% often beat one long wait for 100%. Level 1 can be enough if you drive under about 30 to 40 miles a day and stay plugged in overnight.

Installing a home charger

A Level 2 charger needs a dedicated 240 V circuit. Electrical codes treat EV charging as a continuous load, so the circuit must be rated at 125% of the charger's draw: a 32-amp charger needs a 40-amp circuit, and a 48-amp charger needs a 60-amp circuit and is usually hardwired.

Plug-in units use a 240 V outlet such as a NEMA 14-50 and move with you; hardwired units allow higher power and avoid outlet wear. Installation cost depends on spare capacity in your panel, the distance to your parking spot and how the wiring is routed. An older home may need a panel or service upgrade that costs more than the charger itself.

Use a licensed electrician who does a load calculation first, pull any required permit, and choose a charger listed by a recognized safety testing lab. Ask your utility about EV time-of-use rates or charger rebates. Renters and condo owners should check building rules and whether their state or province has right-to-charge rules.

Watch out: Never run a Level 2 charger through an extension cord, and don't plug one into an old dryer outlet until an electrician confirms the circuit and receptacle are rated for hours of continuous charging.

Connector standards in North America

North America is partway through a change in plug standards, so know which port your car has.

  • J1772 is the standard AC plug for Level 1 and Level 2 on most non-Tesla EVs and most public Level 2 stations.
  • CCS1 adds two large DC pins below the J1772 shape for fast charging and has been the main fast-charging standard for non-Tesla EVs.
  • NACS, the plug Tesla developed, is being standardized by SAE International as J3400. Many automakers have announced a switch to J3400 ports, rolling out from around 2025, and many non-Tesla models can already use parts of the Supercharger network with an adapter or native port.
  • CHAdeMO is an older DC standard on some earlier Japanese-brand EVs, and fewer new stations support it.

Access varies by brand, model year and station, so follow your automaker's guidance and use only approved or safety-certified adapters. The UK and India mainly use Type 2 and CCS2. The Alternative Fuels Data Center explains connector types and maps US stations.

What affects real-world range

The EPA range rating is a fair benchmark for comparing models, but your daily range will move around it.

  • Cold weather. AAA testing cited by fueleconomy.gov found range can fall by about 41% in cold weather with the heater running, mostly because of cabin heating.
  • Highway speed. Drag climbs steeply with speed, so 75 mph uses noticeably more energy than 60 mph. Stop-and-go traffic is often efficient thanks to regenerative braking.
  • Heating and cooling. A heat pump, preconditioning while plugged in and heated seats all reduce the hit.
  • Load. Towing, roof boxes, larger wheels and heavy cargo all cut range, towing dramatically.

Plan on noticeably less than rated range in winter and on fast highways, and arrive at chargers with a 10% to 20% buffer.

What charging costs: a worked example

Multiply your car's efficiency in kWh per 100 miles by your electricity price. The EPA figure is measured from the wall, so it already includes charging losses. For an EV rated at 30 kWh/100 mi with home electricity at $0.16 per kWh:

  • 30 kWh × $0.16 = $4.80 per 100 miles
  • At 12,000 miles a year: 120 × $4.80 = $576 a year
  • A 30 mpg gas car with fuel at $3.50 a gallon costs about $11.67 per 100 miles, or roughly $1,400 a year

Public fast charging changes the math: at $0.45 per kWh, the same EV costs $13.50 per 100 miles, more than the gas car. Owners who charge mostly at home on off-peak rates save the most. Try your own numbers in the EV vs gas calculator.

Incentives

Incentives vary by location and change often. US federal clean vehicle tax credits ended for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025, but some states, utilities and local programs still offer rebates on vehicles, chargers or electricity rates. Search current programs in the AFDC laws and incentives database and on fueleconomy.gov. Canadian programs have also changed in recent years, so confirm before you count on one.

Key takeaway: Home charging is what makes an EV cheap and convenient to own. If you can't charge at home or work, run the numbers at public fast-charging prices before you buy.

Maintenance and battery warranty

EVs skip oil changes, spark plugs, exhaust repairs and most transmission service, but the list isn't empty:

  • Tires often wear faster because EVs are heavy and torquey. Rotate on schedule and keep pressures correct; see tires 101.
  • Brakes last longer thanks to regenerative braking, but light use can let calipers and rotors corrode, especially on salted roads.
  • Fluids and filters still need attention: brake fluid, battery coolant on liquid-cooled packs and cabin air filters.
  • The 12-volt battery still runs the electronics, and a weak one can stop an EV from starting up.

In the US, the most common high-voltage battery warranty is 8 years or 100,000 miles, whichever comes first; in Canada it's commonly 8 years or 160,000 km, and some brands go longer. Many warranties also cover capacity loss below a stated threshold, often around 70%, though the percentage and how it's measured vary. Note the original in-service date and whether coverage transfers to later owners.

Buying a used EV: battery health checks

The battery is the most expensive part of an EV, so verify its condition before you pay.

  • Get a battery state-of-health report from a dealer, the automaker's app or a reputable diagnostic service
  • Compare the full-charge range estimate with the original rating, allowing for temperature and recent driving
  • Confirm how much battery warranty remains by date and mileage
  • Run the VIN through the recall check, since some EVs have had battery-related recalls
  • Test both Level 2 and DC fast charging, and make sure the cable and any adapters are included
  • Ask about charging habits: frequent fast charging and long stretches at 100% can speed up wear

The bottom line

An EV rewards owners who can charge at home: fuel costs fall, maintenance shrinks and the car is full every morning. Before you buy, confirm where you'll charge, which plug your car uses, how much range you'll really get in your climate and how much battery warranty remains.
