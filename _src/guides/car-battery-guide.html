A typical car battery lasts about three to five years, and heat, not cold, does most of the damage. You can check one in two minutes with a cheap multimeter: about 12.6 volts at rest means fully charged, and anything under about 12.4 volts means it needs a charge.

Your owner's manual lists the exact specifications and any special procedures for your car, so check it before you start.

How long car batteries last

Most conventional lead-acid batteries last three to five years, but climate and driving habits move that range a lot:

  • Heat shortens life most. High temperatures speed up internal corrosion and evaporate electrolyte. In hot regions, two to three years is common.
  • Cold exposes the damage. A cold battery delivers less power just as thick, cold oil makes the engine harder to turn, so a battery weakened by summer often fails on the first frosty morning.
  • Short trips and parking drain it. A 10-minute drive may not replace the power used to start, and a car parked for weeks loses charge to clocks, alarms and computers.

Once a battery is about three years old, have it tested each year, ideally before winter. Add that to your maintenance planner so you don't have to remember it, and see our car maintenance schedule for where battery checks fit alongside oil, brakes and filters.

Warning signs of a weak battery

  • Slow, labored cranking, especially on cold mornings
  • Clicking when you turn the key or press start, with little or no cranking
  • Headlights or interior lights that dim when the engine starts
  • Electrical oddities, such as a clock that resets or a flickering screen
  • Start-stop that no longer shuts the engine off at lights, since many cars disable it when the battery is weak
  • The battery or charging warning light
  • White or blue-green crust on the terminals, a swollen case or a rotten-egg smell

A swollen case or sulfur smell means the battery is damaged or overcharging; don't charge or jump it, replace it. A battery light that comes on while driving usually points to the charging system, often the alternator, rather than the battery.

How to test a car battery

Resting voltage with a multimeter

Set a digital multimeter to DC volts and touch the red probe to the positive terminal and the black probe to the negative. For an accurate reading, the engine should have been off for a few hours so the surface charge from driving has faded.

Resting voltageApproximate chargeWhat to do
12.6 V or higherFullNothing; load test if starting problems continue.
About 12.4 VAbout 75%Recharge with a charger or a longer drive.
About 12.2 VAbout 50%Charge soon; deep discharges shorten battery life.
12.0 V or lowerDischargedCharge fully and retest. If it won't hold a charge, replace it.

With the engine running, most charging systems hold roughly 13.5 to 14.7 volts. A reading near 12.6 or lower suggests the alternator isn't charging, although some newer cars vary charging voltage on purpose.

Load testing

Voltage shows charge, not strength. A worn battery can read 12.6 volts and still collapse when the starter draws hundreds of amps. A load or conductance test checks how well it delivers current against its rating. Shops do this quickly, and many auto parts stores in the US and Canada test batteries free.

How to jump-start a car safely

Check your owner's manual first. Some cars have remote jump points under the hood, and many hybrids and EVs shouldn't be used to jump another vehicle. Both cars should be off, in Park or Neutral, with parking brakes on.

  1. Park the working car close to the dead one without the cars touching.
  2. Connect one red clamp to the dead battery's positive (+) terminal.
  3. Connect the other red clamp to the good battery's positive (+) terminal.
  4. Connect one black clamp to the good battery's negative (−) terminal.
  5. Connect the last black clamp to bare, unpainted metal on the dead car's engine or chassis, away from the battery.
  6. Start the working car and let it run for a few minutes.
  7. Start the dead car. If it won't start after a few tries a few minutes apart, stop and call for help.
  8. Remove the cables in reverse order: black from the dead car's metal, black from the good battery, red from the good battery, then red from the formerly dead battery.

Keep the clamps apart while any are connected. Afterward, drive for 20 to 30 minutes or use a charger, then get the battery tested. One jump may just mean a drained battery; repeated jumps mean it's failing.

Watch out: Lead-acid batteries can release hydrogen gas that a spark can ignite, which is why the last clamp goes on bare metal away from the battery. Never jump a battery that is frozen, cracked, swollen or leaking.

Portable jump starters

A portable lithium jump starter lets you start your car without a second vehicle. Most are paperback-sized and clamp on the same way, following their own instructions, and many have reverse-polarity protection and a USB port for your phone. Choose one rated for your engine size, which matters most for big V8s and diesels, and recharge it every few months.

Replacing a car battery

Match these specifications from your owner's manual or the label on your current battery:

  • Group size: a code such as 24F, 35, 48 or 65 that sets the battery's dimensions and terminal positions. The wrong size may not fit the tray or reach the cables.
  • Cold cranking amps (CCA): the current the battery delivers at 0°F (−18°C). Meet or exceed the original rating, especially in cold climates.
  • Type: conventional flooded batteries are cheapest. AGM (absorbed glass mat) batteries are sealed, cope better with frequent cycling and are standard in many start-stop cars. If your car came with AGM, replace it with AGM.

Check the date code, a sticker or stamp showing when the battery was made, and choose one under about six months old. Most batteries come with a free-replacement period, and some add a prorated period after that.

Many modern cars, especially European models, have a battery management system that must be told a new battery is fitted. This registration is done with a scan tool. Skip it and the car may keep charging the new battery as though it were the old one, shortening its life. Some cars also need windows or other settings reset after a battery change.

In the US, a conventional flooded battery commonly runs roughly $100 to $250 and an AGM battery $200 to $350 or more, plus fitting if a shop does it. It's a regular running cost worth including in your total cost of ownership.

  • CCA meets or exceeds the original rating
  • Same type as the original (AGM replaced with AGM)
  • Date code is recent
  • Battery registered if your car requires it
  • Old battery returned and core charge refunded

Recycling your old battery

Car batteries contain lead and sulfuric acid, so they never go in household trash or curbside recycling. They're also among the most recycled consumer products, since nearly all the lead and plastic can be reused. The EPA's battery recycling guidance says to return them to a battery retailer or a household hazardous waste program.

Many US retailers add a refundable core charge, often $10 to $20, if you don't hand in your old battery, which you get back when you return it. Take-back rules vary by state, province and country, so check your local requirements.

EVs and hybrids still have a 12-volt battery

Electric cars and hybrids carry a small low-voltage battery alongside the big traction pack. It runs the computers, locks, lights and the switches that connect the high-voltage battery. If it dies, the car may not unlock or turn on, even with a full main pack.

It's charged from the main battery rather than an alternator, but it still wears out. Some newer EVs use a lithium-ion low-voltage battery, sometimes 16 volts, so check your manual before buying a replacement or connecting a jump starter. Our EV ownership guide covers the rest of EV upkeep.

Key takeaway: Test your battery yearly once it passes three years old, keep a jump starter or cables in the car, and when you replace it, match the group size, CCA rating and battery type your car was designed for.

The bottom line

Car batteries rarely fail without warning. Watch for slow cranking, check the resting voltage, and get a load test before winter once the battery is a few years old. Learn the jump-start order before you need it, match the original specifications when you replace it, and recycle the old one.
